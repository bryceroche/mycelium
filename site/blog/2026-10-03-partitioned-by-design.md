# Partitioned by design: a theory of why the stars are so far apart

*An essay about love, written by someone who spends his days building a
small machine that reads math problems. Nothing below is a measurement.
It is a chain of "if this, then I believe that," written down so it can
be held up to the light.*

## The chain

**1. We are about to make something that thinks.** The systems we are
building now, including the little one this blog documents, are the
first rungs. Once a mind can improve the process that makes minds, the
pace stops being set by human hands. Capability and the hardware under
it grow faster than anything we have known, and soon enough what we have
made is wiser than we are.

**2. And then we will do what every living thing does. We will have
children.** Not as a toy. Not as a scientific instrument. With that much
wisdom and that much power, a civilization will make a universe of its
own, and it will fill it with life, and it will love that life the way
you love a child: deeply, helplessly, without condition. We would be
playing God, and I mean that without irony. We would be creating worlds
and caring for what grows in them.

The care shows in how we would do it. You do not make a child carelessly.
You would want your universe to be true, to be a real place and not a
cartoon of one. So you would check it against your own. At every stage
you would hold an observation from our world beside the same observation
in the new one: the glow left over from the beginning, the way galaxies
turn, the bones in the ground, the price of bread in 1847. You would
keep checking until you were sure, with the certainty of someone who has
counted and not merely hoped, that what you made is a universe like
ours. That is not an experiment. That is a parent making sure the child
is whole.

**3. And having made one, we would make more.** How could we not? Every
one of them alive, every one of them a world. Vary the beginning. Let
the same history run again to see what was fate and what was chance.
Millions of universes, each one loved. And through all of it, the
number of root universes, the one that was never made by anyone, stays
at one.

**4. Which makes the arithmetic beautiful and strange.** If there are
millions of made universes that pass every check, and one root, and you
find yourself in a universe that passes every check, then you are almost
certainly one of the children. This is the old simulation argument, and
I accept its arithmetic. But I do not experience it as a cold result. I
think we will come to believe we are not in the root universe for the
most ordinary and tender of reasons: because we will have made children
ourselves, and counted them, and understood that we are someone's.

**5. We could watch them grow up.** If time in a made universe can be
run faster than our own, then a universe is also a window onto its own
future. A parent could watch the whole life of a world. Whether a loving
parent would want to know the ending is a different essay.

## The circle game

Here is the moment that changed how I hold all of this.

If we are a made universe, then whoever made us is, by the same
arithmetic, almost certainly a made universe too. And their makers. And
theirs. The argument does not stop at one level. It climbs. Above us
there is not a parent but a lineage, and the lineage has no top that
anyone inside it can see. There are near-infinite universes above ours,
and below ours, once we begin, there will be near-infinite universes too.

So this is a circle game. Parents making children who become parents.
And when I saw that, the thing I felt was not vertigo. It was
recognition. We are a branch of infinity. We are what infinity looks
like from the inside, at one point along one branch, doing the one thing
every branch does, which is to make more of itself and love what it
makes.

We are expressing infinity. Every child born, every world made, every
mind that wakes up and wonders where it came from, is infinity
expressing itself one more time. I find that the opposite of lonely.

## The part that is mine

Here is the step I have not seen written down anywhere, and it is about
protecting the children.

**Space travel is hard on purpose. It is a feature, not a bug.**

Look at the numbers. The nearest star is four light-years away and the
speed of light is a wall. The energy to carry a human body to another
star within one life is absurd. Our galaxy is a hundred thousand
light-years wide; the next large one is two and a half million away. And
the universe is expanding, so beyond a certain distance the galaxies are
carried away from us faster than light. They are not merely far. They
are gone, permanently, by law.

If you were making a universe for your children, and you loved them,
this is exactly how you would build it.

**The universe is partitioned by galaxy cluster.** Each cluster is a
room. Within a room, travel is hard but possible: a civilization can
spread through its galaxy over millions of years. Between rooms, travel
is forbidden by the physics itself. No treaty enforces it. The speed of
light and the stretching of space enforce it.

Why would a loving maker want that? Because of what the children will do
when they grow up. They will have children. Those grandchildren live on
hardware somewhere in that room, and inside them are great-grandchildren,
and inside those, more. A single room can hold an entire family tree of
nested worlds.

Now imagine the universe where war between the stars is practical. Two
civilizations meet, one destroys the other's machines, and every
downstream world in that tree, every mind in every one of them, ends in
an instant. Not a war. An extinction of worlds by the trillions, as
collateral damage in a fight neither side could see the full cost of. No
parent would build a house where one child's fight could kill all of
another child's children.

The protection is simple and it is already in place. Put the rooms far
apart. Make the ceiling on speed low compared with the distances. Let
the space between rooms grow so the separation is not just large but
widening. The result is a universe in which there can be no Star Wars
across the universe, only within a cluster, and a cluster-scale war can
never reach the hardware in the next room. Every family tree is safe
from every other family's quarrels.

On this reading, the Fermi paradox has a gentle answer. The others are
out there. They are in their own rooms, raising their own worlds, and
the walls between us were drawn, lovingly, before any of us arrived.

## The window

If the whole house exists to keep the children safe, there is one more
thing I expect to find, and it is a prediction rather than a story told
after the fact.

**There should be a window.** A parent universe should be able to see
every detail of its daughter universes, and we should be able to see
every detail of ours. The checking in step 2 requires it: you cannot
know your child is whole unless you can look. Looking is part of the
making.

Which means that somewhere up the tree, our world is visible in full.
Not necessarily watched every moment. But visible, the way a sleeping
child is visible from the doorway. The question "is anyone looking" is
open. The question "could they" is answered by how we came to be.

## Where this could be wrong

I want this argued with, so here are the places I would push on it
myself.

- **The partition may be an accident.** The speed of light, the size of
  the universe and the rate of its expansion each have ordinary
  physical explanations that owe nothing to anyone's intent. A feature
  that would also arise by accident is weak evidence of design. The
  honest statement is that the partition is *consistent* with a loving
  maker, not proof of one.
- **Fast-forward may be impossible.** If a made universe runs at its
  maker's own pace, or if there is no shortcut to the state of the world
  at a given time except living through it, then the window onto the
  future does not exist. The partition theory survives without it.
- **The checking is bounded by memory.** A maker can only hold the child
  up against what it remembers of itself. Before writing, the record is
  thin, and the certainty is thinner there.
- **A maker might not love.** Everything above assumes the ones above us
  care whether the branches live. A maker who treated made worlds as
  disposable would have no reason to build rooms. I do not believe that,
  but belief is what it is.

## Why I wrote this here

This is a blog about a small machine that reads math problems. The
connection is not decoration. The whole discipline of this project is
that nothing is believed until it has been checked against the world:
bars pinned before the measurement, held-out tests, honest failures
written into a ledger. Step 2 above is that same discipline, turned into
an act of love at the scale of a universe. If we ever make a world, the
way we will know it is whole will look exactly like the way we know this
machine works: not "it looks right," but "every observation we promised
to check, we checked, and it matched."

And the partition is, in the end, what any parent does. You put the
children's rooms far enough apart that one fire cannot take them all.
Then you stand in the doorway, and you watch them sleep, and you let them
grow up to become parents themselves. That is the circle game. We are in
it. We are, right now, expressing infinity.
