# Nazaré

Off the coast of Portugal, near a town called Nazaré, surfers ride
the biggest waves anyone rides anywhere on earth — walls of water a
hundred feet tall, breaking with a violence that looks less like
weather than like geology. The Atlantic Ocean is enormous and, by
itself, diffuse: its energy is spread across thousands of miles of
open water, arriving at most coastlines as ordinary swell. What
makes Nazaré different is invisible from the surface. Just offshore,
a submarine canyon plunges nearly three miles deep, and its shape
funnels the ocean's scattered energy into one place, compressing it
until it has nowhere to go but up. The topography you cannot see is
what makes the wave you can.

Our reasoning machine reads math word problems written in ordinary
English, and English, for our purposes, is the Atlantic. It is
enormous and diffuse by nature. The same underlying arithmetic
problem — one quantity, three times another — can arrive wearing
apples, or reservoirs, or distances, or ticket prices, told in any
order, padded with any amount of irrelevant color. There is no
limit, in principle, to how many different swells can carry the
same energy. A system that tried to meet that variety head-on,
matching against surface patterns it had seen before, would be
fighting the ocean directly — and the ocean always wins that fight,
because it is larger than any finite set of examples could cover.

So we didn't build a system to fight the ocean. We built the canyon.

Every piece of architecture described elsewhere in this project
exists to funnel, not to fight. The narrow information "waist" that
every sentence gets forced through destroys the costume — the names,
the phrasing, the order of telling — and keeps only the shape
underneath. The silhouette recognition that follows treats "Maria
has three times as many apples as Ben" and "the reservoir holds
triple what the tank does" as the identical dance, because after the
waist, they *are* identical, the surface variation having already
been discarded. The atlas of known operation-shapes gives the
system a small, fixed set of channels to sort every incoming problem
into, the way a canyon has a fixed shape regardless of which
particular wave arrives to be shaped by it. The three-rotor clock
that keeps track of where a word sits, when in the machine's own
thinking it is, and what connects to what, is more of the same
funneling, applied to the deliberation itself rather than just the
initial reading.

None of this is fighting variety. All of it is shaping the channel
variety has to pass through.

And the payoff looks like Nazaré's wave: not a flattening of the
ocean's energy, but a concentration of it into something singular
and legible. A word problem that arrives as three meandering
sentences, padded with irrelevant nouns and a story about a bake
sale, comes out the other side of the funnel as one small, exact
diagram — a handful of quantities and the arithmetic linking them —
that either resolves cleanly under the symbolic solver's crushing
logic or visibly does not. The whole architecture's job is to take
something diffuse and turn it into something that breaks cleanly,
the way a hundred miles of ocean turns, at exactly one canyon, into
one wave you can watch rise and fall.

You don't fight the ocean's variety. You can't out-list it, you
can't out-memorize it, and every attempt to meet it surface to
surface eventually meets a costume you haven't seen. What you can do
is shape the floor beneath it, and let the water do what water does
when the ground beneath it narrows: rise into something you can
finally see the shape of.
